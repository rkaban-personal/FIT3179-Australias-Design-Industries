A field guide to Australia’s design industries

Pull up a chair. Look around the room. Someone shaped the things you see—and the screen you are reading.

But how do we count the work behind them? Follow the people, the studios and the applications to protect what products look like. Each tells a different part of the story.

One word.
Three ways to count.

A garment can lead us to its designer, the business that employs them, or an application to protect its appearance. These are different questions, with different answers.

The person Occupation
What someone does in their main job. A graphic designer can work inside a bank.
The business Industry
Its main economic activity. A design business can also employ accountants and managers.
The application Design right
A filing to protect a product’s appearance. It is neither a person nor a business.

Keep these lenses separate. Adding their totals would count different things as if they were the same.

Start with the person behind it.

A room, a garment and a screen ask different things of a designer. The occupation lens helps us see those differences. First, a historical glimpse; then a separate 2021 snapshot of working patterns.

A glimpse of an earlier expansion

Historical comparison · 2006 & 2011

All seven selected occupations grew between these two censuses. Web designers rose from 3,486 to 5,015, while graphic designers remained the largest group shown.

This historical window shows how the selected roles changed within one classification. It is not evidence of today’s growth.

Working patterns differ by role

Workforce snapshot · 2021 census

Graphic and interior design have part-time shares around 37%. UI/UX—user interface and user experience design—has a much lower published share, 11.4%.

Read the two charts separately. The historical ANZSCO counts are not a continuous series with the newer OSCA estimates. Female is the source’s Census sex category, not a complete measure of gender identity. Part-time shares exclude people away from work in the reference week; COVID restrictions also affected the 2021 snapshot. The scatterplot shows association, not a cause.

The job title does not tell you the workplace

Industry profiles · 2021 Census

Designers work beyond specialist studios. In the published profiles, 22.8% of industrial designers worked in manufacturing, while 22.2% of multimedia designers worked in education and training.

Read each line from top to bottom. Every horizontal axis uses the same 0–100% scale. A line describes one occupation across five selected employer industries; it does not trace people moving between them.

Three example profiles are emphasised; all eight remain visible.

The person is only one lens. Next, we change the question: what can we learn from the businesses themselves?

Now step into the studio.

A designer’s occupation tells us about their work. A business’s industry tells us about its main activity. At June 2025, Australia had 17,498 businesses in “Other Specialised Design Services” (ANZSIC 6924): a class covering graphic, fashion and interior design services, among others. Architecture, engineering design and computer-system design sit elsewhere. Industrial design services sit within the broader engineering-services class 6923, so the chair motif should not be read as a boundary for class 6924.

New South Wales has the highest concentration

Rate choropleth · June 2025

It has 7.38 specialised design businesses per 1,000 businesses overall. Normalising by all businesses makes jurisdictions of different sizes more comparable.

Notice the denominator. Darker shading means more specialised design businesses relative to all businesses, not simply a larger state.

Two states hold about two-thirds of the count

Proportional symbols · June 2025

New South Wales and Victoria together account for 11,696 businesses, or 66.8% of the national total. Circle area represents the count.

A business is assigned to its main state, not to every place it operates. Detailed ABS cells are perturbed for confidentiality. Both maps use ABS ASGS 2021 boundaries, simplified for display with small islands omitted. NSW = New South Wales; VIC = Victoria; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; WA = Western Australia; TAS = Tasmania; NT = Northern Territory; ACT = Australian Capital Territory.

Seven in ten specialised design businesses are non-employing

Business-size mosaic · June 2025

The share is 69.3%, compared with 52.4% in architecture and 59.9% in advertising. These are three separate business classes, chosen for comparison.

Read this mosaic in two directions: column width = businesses in the class; coloured height = its employment-size mix.

A small net gain hides much larger movement

Business turnover · 2024–2025

Specialised design services added a net 181 businesses. Behind that change were 2,534 entries and 2,353 exits.

The national change hides different state experiences

State exit-rate distributions · 2024–2025

Specialised design’s published exit rate ranged from 8.2% in Tasmania to 21.3% in the Northern Territory. The Territory began with just 75 businesses, so its high rate describes a small base.

Each labelled dot is one state or territory. The box contains the middle half of the eight rates; the line inside is their median. Whiskers reach the lowest and highest rates. Every jurisdiction has equal weight.

These figures describe a defined set of businesses. What changes when the definition of design becomes wider?

Some design work hides in plain sight.

The chair brought us to a designer. The studio brought us to a business class. Neither captures every place design contributes value. An older IP Australia study widened the frame to architecture, software and design-related supply chains.

Services, architecture and digital activities dominate this estimate

Historical economic benchmark

The largest sector contributes A$44.534 billion of the report’s A$67.507 billion combined estimate. The rectangle areas show published contributions; lighter leaves show designers embedded outside the defined design industries.

This is not Australia’s current design-industry revenue. Values are in constant 2019 Australian dollars. Industry contributions average financial years 2008–09 to 2016–17; embedded contributions average 2011 and 2016 and are experimental. The treemap reproduces the report’s mixed-period benchmark.

The classroom opens another door.

The story continues in the classroom, where design connects creative arts, built-environment courses and digital study. The figures below describe these broad fields, rather than a count of design students or a pathway into design employment.

IT commencements almost doubled from 2015

Indexed heatmap · 2012–2024

By 2024, domestic commencements were 91.4% higher in Information Technology, 23.4% higher in Architecture and Building and 21.8% lower in Creative Arts. An index compares relative change rather than field size.

Colours compare each year with 2015. The headline comparison stays 2015–2024.

An idea can cross a border.

Return to the shape of the chair or the cut of the garment. A design right can protect a product’s visual appearance. Applications reveal one formal part of design activity, including the interest of overseas applicants in protection in Australia.

Overseas applicants drive most of the increase

Annual applications · 2016–2025

Applications reached 10,296 in 2025. Non-residents accounted for 7,218, or 70.1%. Resident filings were 3,078, compared with 2,749 in 2016.

The United States and China lead the published origins

International filing relationships · 2025

The five largest overseas origins account for 5,205 filings—72.1% of non-resident applications. Another 2,013 overseas applications are outside this map.

All five published origins are highlighted.

The last twist is in the label.

Just when a count seems settled, its definition can change. Australia’s newer occupation classification splits some categories and combines parts of others. A new name does not necessarily mean a new job.

Follow the definitions before comparing the numbers

Unweighted correspondence network

For example, the older Industrial Designer category partly corresponds to both Industrial Designer and Textile Designer in OSCA. These are official conceptual links, not movements of workers.

The room looks the same.
The questions have changed.

The chair is still a chair. But behind it, we can now ask about a person’s work, a business’s main activity, or a protected appearance. The same habit travels well—from Australia to a classroom in Malaysia.

Before trusting a headline, ask: what is counted, in which year, and compared with what?

Why the years differ

Each dataset uses its appropriate release and reference period. Detailed 2025 business-class counts are paired with 2025 denominators; the 2026 industry-class cube was not available at audit. Workforce data refer to the 2021 Census, even when recoded to a 2024 classification. Education ends in 2024 and IP activity in 2025. Historical comparisons are explicitly labelled.

Map and chart choices

Rates use colour; counts use proportional area; filing relationships use proportional line width. A new parallel-coordinates view compares published industry shares, and a box plot summarises eight state exit rates with every observation visible. Maps use verified ABS and Natural Earth geography. No arbitrary business locations or inferred sub-state counts are shown. Basemaps do not endorse sovereignty claims.

How to read and check the data

Every chart has a keyboard-accessible data table and CSV download. Hover or touch a mark for more detail; essential findings are also written in the page. Controls change an index baseline or highlight a profile or filing origin. They never hide a chapter. Labels, numbers and line styles support colour-vision differences. Text summaries and semantic tables provide a reading route without seeing the charts.

Readable Vega / Vega-Lite specifications are linked under each chart. Counts retain published rounding or confidentiality adjustment. No missing source observations are filled or smoothed, and no worker flows are invented. Box-plot quartiles are calculated from the published jurisdiction rates. Small-size categories may be difficult to see in the mosaic or treemap; their exact values remain in the tables.
